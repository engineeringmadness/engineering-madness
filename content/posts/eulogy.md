The so-called AI revolution is everywhere now, but for me it's personal: it's the turn of internal tooling software to suffer the repercussions. Internal tools are what I've worked on since the start of my career. What defines this world is fast, sometimes unreasonable deadlines and non-existent scale, with most software projects having ten thousand users at best. There is a lot of obfuscation and a lack of clarity around requirements when it comes to functional processes.

Funnily enough, this AI coding revolution that has people hating their jobs has nothing to do with AI coding agents at all. AI is simply the medium for the thing that has really brought about the end of programming.

### Why I'm here

To understand how we got here, we need to understand the factors that enabled AI coding agents to take over the jobs of programmers, especially when it comes to the world of internal software. Three things stand out.

1. Quality stopped mattering - Having worked in this space for many years, I've come to realize that a vast majority of such internal software is below average. Think line-of-business apps, mostly CRUD apps along with workflow engines, data platforms, reporting solutions, etc. They have been written to meet deadlines fast rather than to survive for years in production. The codebase is often completely illogical in its nature—the kind of stuff you look at and wonder what the person was thinking when they wrote it. But the real shift was that defects started being just numbers on a JIRA board: whether a release was greenlit with ten defects or a hundred or five hundred stopped making a difference.
2. Releases became marketing gimmicks - Release mails are sent with fervor even if the functionality being released doesn't actually work. Long-term foundation work is ignored; the only thing that matters is how shiny the release announcement is. Deliverables stopped being measured by their impact or by what they enabled downstream, and instead became about what seemed the shiniest and most complicated.
3. Leadership hates engineers - Most people don't realize this, but executives hate engineers. Good engineers take an insane amount of money out of budgets, are highly opinionated to the point of being insufferable, and question every requirement and decision. If given a magic solution that could do 80% of what an engineer does (or at least appear to do so), these executives would fire a majority of their engineers gleefully.

Put those three together, and the current enthusiasm starts to make sense. The concept of dark software factories—a.k.a. Claude Code being used to automate software development end to end, from creating tickets to writing specs to implementation of features—is basically a wet dream for these executives. I have heard executives espouse vibe coding and literally say stuff like the language or framework the AI uses does not matter. The architecture it comes up with does not matter. We should apparently treat the code generated by AI as a black box now, with no need to understand it. I 100% DISAGREE WITH THIS.

![](/rant2.png)

### What do I feel

I feel sad, because the writing really is on the wall.

I feel angry towards the people in management for pretending that the vibe-coded AI slop is all hunky-dory just so they can appear to be cool. Will programming die permanently? I don't know the answer to that question, but I do know this: it's dying for the time being. Maybe there is a future where these executives go neck-deep in vibe coding, make such a mess that they get fired, and the engineers make a comeback, or maybe that doesn't happen.

AI has never been the problem. Software has evolved in many ways over the years, and the way we develop it has evolved too. I think it's time for me to find a company that treats AI as an amazing tool rather than a way to show off and avoid having engineers.
